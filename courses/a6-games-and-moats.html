Games and moats

Five sessions have treated the price as a message and the buyer as the one who answers it. Tonight the other seller answers too. When a rival can see your price and reply, the best move depends on what they will do, and what they will do depends on what they think you will do. That loop is a game, and the smallest one, two players and two moves, explains why two sellers who would both be better off holding their prices end up cutting them. From there the session goes to the claims that get made about size: that a network's value grows with the square of its members, which has a published rebuttal; that switching costs are a moat, which is true until you look for the door; and that the winner takes all, when on most marketplaces the winner takes most and the rest multi-home.

Part 0

Where we start

Every number so far has had one decision-maker behind it. Foundations session 2's elasticity, between -1.5 and -2.2 on the 203 Olist products that can be read, says how buyers answer when one seller moves a price and everybody else does whatever they were doing. It does not say what the seller across the shelf does next, because no panel of past prices contains the rival's reply. Game theory is the habit of writing the reply down before you move. It is also where the popular stories about "moats" come from, and most of those stories are claims wearing the costume of laws. Tonight separates the mechanism, which is real, from the slogan, which is often not.

Part 1 · covers strategic interaction, the 2x2 game

When the other player moves too

6 min live

Two sellers list the same kind of product on the same marketplace. Each can hold their price or cut it. If both hold, both do fine. If one cuts, the buyers slide across the shelf and the cutter does very well while the holder does badly. If both cut, both sell the same units as before at a lower price and both do worse than if both had held. Write those four outcomes in a square and you have the prisoner's dilemma, and the square tells you something uncomfortable: each seller cuts, whatever the other does, and both end up in the cell neither wanted.

LiveThe prisoner's dilemma on a shelf: dominant strategy and the cell nobody wanted4 min▶

A game is any situation where what you get depends on what somebody else chooses as well as on what you choose. A strategy is a full plan of what you will do. A strategy is dominant when it is your best plan no matter which plan the other player picks. Most games have no dominant strategy, which is why they are hard. The prisoner's dilemma is famous because each player has one, and following it makes both worse off.

Read the table by columns. Suppose Duarte holds: Beira earns 100 by holding and 130 by cutting, so cut. Suppose Duarte cuts: Beira earns 60 by holding and 80 by cutting, so cut again. Cut wins in both columns; it is dominant. Duarte, reading the rows, reaches the same conclusion. Both cut, both earn 80, and the 100 each would have earned by holding is gone. Nobody made a mistake. Each played the best reply to what the other would do, and the outcome is worse for both than a pair of moves neither can hold on their own.

The data habit this gives you is the one Foundations session 2's number cannot: an elasticity says how buyers answer, and only buyers. Before any price move, write the rival's reply in the next cell, and ask whether the number you computed still describes the world after they make it.

Self-studyRepeated play, and why a standoff sometimes holds after all3 min▶

The table is played once. Real sellers face each other every month, and that changes the arithmetic without changing the table. If Beira cuts this month, Duarte can match next month, and then both sit at 80 for as long as they both remember. A seller who expects to be on the shelf next year weighs 130 now against 80 forever, and holding starts to look better than the single-round table says. Repetition is what lets two sellers hold a price without ever agreeing to, and it is why the standoff you can see on many shelves is quieter than the dilemma predicts.

Two conditions have to be true for the quiet to last. The rival must be able to see the cut, which on a marketplace with public prices they can, and the rival must be expected to stay, which on a marketplace with 3,095 sellers is not always true for any one of them. When either condition fails the game collapses back to the single round, and the cut comes.

Real world

Beira Goods and Duarte Supply, both constructed. Beira, the small marketplace seller from the earlier sessions, lists a bath towel at R$ 100. Duarte, an invented rival, lists a near-identical one at the same price. Beira's analyst brings the within-product elasticity from the real panel, about -1.8, and says a cut will move units. She is right about the buyers. What she has not written down is the next cell: Duarte sees the cut on the same page the buyers do, and can match it by lunchtime. Every price in this story is invented. The fact that a rival's reply is not in any elasticity is not.

Part 2 · covers network effects as a claim

Metcalfe's law, and the rebuttal

5 min live

A network effect is real: a marketplace with more buyers is worth more to a seller, and a marketplace with more sellers is worth more to a buyer. What is not settled is how much more. The popular claim, Metcalfe's law, says the value of a network grows with the square of its members, because that is how the number of possible pairs grows. Briscoe, Odlyzko and Tilly published a rebuttal in IEEE Spectrum in 2006 titled "Metcalfe's Law is Wrong", arguing that value grows in proportion to n log n instead. The two laws agree that bigger is better. They disagree by a lot about how much a doubling is worth, and that difference is the whole of a valuation argument.

Liven squared, n log n, and what each predicts when the network doubles3 min▶

Metcalfe's law counts pairs. With n members there are about n squared over two possible connections, so if every connection is worth the same, value grows as n squared. Doubling the members quadruples the value. It is a clean story and it is why the phrase "network effects" turns up in pitch decks as if it were arithmetic.

The rebuttal attacks the assumption that every connection is worth the same. Briscoe, Odlyzko and Tilly argued that a member's connections fall off in value the way most things do: the most useful link is worth a lot, the tenth is worth a tenth of that, the hundredth a hundredth. Sum a series like that and each member gets about log n of value, so the network as a whole is worth about n log n. Doubling from a thousand members then gives 2.2 times the value, not four. That is pure arithmetic on the two claims, and it is the only number on this page about network size.

The honest position is that both are claims. The mechanism, that a bigger network is worth more to each member, is real and is why marketplaces exist. The exponent is not a law of nature and has never been measured on the marketplace whose data runs through these sessions. When somebody quotes a square, ask them which of the two they mean, and whether they can show a single connection that is worth the same as the first one.

Self-studyDirect and indirect network effects, and the marketplace as a two-sided one3 min▶

A network effect is direct when a member gains from more members of the same kind: a messaging app is worth more to you when your friends are on it. It is indirect when a member gains from more members of the other kind: a buyer gains from more sellers, a seller from more buyers, and a seller gains nothing, or less than nothing, from another seller. A marketplace runs almost entirely on the indirect kind, which is why it is called two-sided, and why it has a chicken-and-egg problem at birth: nobody sells where nobody buys, and nobody buys where nobody sells.

The distinction matters for the claim in Part 2. Pairs of sellers are not connections anyone values, so counting all pairs among all members overstates a marketplace's network even before the log n argument arrives. The connections that carry value cross the platform from one side to the other. Deep-dive session 5 draws both sides with the real counts and does the arithmetic at ten times the members rather than two.

Real world

The marketplace has two sides, and the counts are lopsided. The Olist dataset behind every session carries 3,095 sellers and 96,096 customer ids, counted in the RFM course on the same export, and 32,216 products with at least one sale. A seller's network is the buyers, thirty-odd of them for each seller on average; a buyer's network is the sellers. Nobody on either side gains from a pair on their own side, and the 203 products that can be read for elasticity are a reminder that most of what a big number contains is quiet: most products never repriced, or sold too little to say. Size is a fact. What it is worth is the claim.

Part 3 · covers switching costs, winner-take-most, multi-homing

Walls, doors, and the winner who takes most

4 min live

A switching cost is everything a customer, or a seller, would have to give up or redo to move from where they are to a rival. It is a wall, and walls are where the word "moat" comes from. But every wall has a door, because the rival's whole business is building one: a catalogue importer, a contract buyout, a migration team. And on many marketplaces the door is already open, because the seller lists on two platforms at once. That is multi-homing, and it is the reason the winner takes most and not all.

LiveWhat a switching cost is made of, and why multi-homing lowers the wall4 min▶

Write a switching cost as a list and it stops being a slogan. For a seller on a marketplace: the hours to re-upload a catalogue, the reviews that stay behind, the search rank earned over months of sales, a contract's notice period, the team's habits. Each brick has a price, and the sum is the wall. The incumbent builds the wall; the rival builds the door: an importer for the catalogue, a launch promotion that stands in for the missing rank, a buyout of the notice period. The wall's height is therefore not a property of the market. It is an ongoing contest.

Winner-take-most is what network effects and switching costs together produce when they work: the biggest platform is worth the most to join and the most expensive to leave, so it keeps growing. The limit on it is multi-homing. A seller who lists on two marketplaces at once has no wall to climb; they are already on both sides. A buyer with two apps on their phone is the same. Where multi-homing is cheap, the biggest platform still wins the most, but the second and third survive on the same sellers and the same buyers, and "all" never happens.

The test for any moat claim is one question: where is the door, and who is building it? If nobody can name the door, the claim is probably true for now. If the door is already open, as it is wherever multi-homing is common, the moat is a wall with a queue walking through it.

Self-studyVickrey's auction in one page: why bidding your true value is the dominant strategy2 min▶

Part 1's word, dominant, has one famous application beyond the dilemma. In a sealed-bid auction where the highest bidder wins but pays the second highest bid, the best thing to do is bid exactly what the item is worth to you, whatever anybody else bids. Bid more, and the only time it changes anything is when you win at a price above your value and lose money. Bid less, and the only time it changes anything is when you lose an item you would have paid for. Truthful bidding is dominant. That result is Vickrey, in the Journal of Finance, 1961, volume 16, issue 1, pages 8 to 37.

Why it belongs in a session on games: it is the cleanest case of a mechanism designed so that the game has a dominant strategy, which removes the guessing. Most auctions people actually meet, including the ones that price ads, are not designed that way, and deep-dive session 6 shows what goes wrong when they are not.

Self-studyCommitment and credibility; when a data advantage compounds and when it does not3 min▶

Commitment. In the dilemma, Beira would love to promise "I will hold if you hold". The promise is worthless because holding is not Beira's best reply once Duarte holds. A commitment only changes a game if it is credible: if breaking it costs more than keeping it. A public price-match guarantee is a credible commitment to cut, which oddly makes cutting less attractive for the rival, because they know the reply arrives automatically. The lesson is that words move nothing; what moves a game is a change in the payoffs behind the words.

Data as a moat. The most common moat claim in a data business is "we have more data, so our model is better, so we win more customers, so we get more data". The loop is real at the start. It stops being real for a mechanical reason: learning curves flatten. Early rows teach a model the common cases and accuracy climbs fast; later rows are mostly common cases again, and what is left to learn is rare. Each doubling of data buys less than the doubling before it. Once both you and a rival are on the flat part, a rival with a fraction of your data is a few points behind, not a generation behind. Where the loop does keep compounding is where the data itself is hard to get, or where it is tied to a switching cost that keeps the customer producing it for you. That is a wall again, and the door question applies. Deep-dive session 5 fits the curve to constructed data and reads where it flattens; no published exponent is quoted here because none is on file for these pages.

Real world

Beira's 400 reviews, constructed. Beira Goods has 40 products and, say, 400 reviews earned over two years on its marketplace. A rival platform offers a launch fee waiver and an importer that moves the catalogue in an afternoon. The catalogue brick is gone; the reviews brick is not, and it is the one buyers look at. Beira's answer is the multi-homer's: list on both, keep the reviews where they are, and let the second platform earn its own. The wall never got climbed. It got walked around, which is what most sellers on most marketplaces do. The numbers are invented; the walk-around is how switching costs are usually beaten.

Build-along

The pricing standoff, worked on paper

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code tonight. Three worksheets, each a few lines, and each one turns a slogan from the session into something you have written down for your own situation. The first is the 2x2 for a constructed pricing standoff, with the payoffs invented so that the reasoning is the point. The second and third are about your own network effects and your own switching costs, which are the two things every moat claim you will ever hear is made of.

Worksheet 1 · Write the 2x2 for a standoff. Take Beira and Duarte, or two sellers you know. Invent the four payoffs, and say so at the top of the page. Then read it by columns: for each thing the rival might do, which of your moves pays more? If the same move wins both columns, it is dominant, and you already know where the shelf lands.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Foundations session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Two sellers can each hold or cut. Both holding pays 100 each; one cutting alone pays the cutter 130 and the holder 60; both cutting pays 80 each. What happens, and why?

A dominant strategy is best whatever the other player does. The payoffs are invented, but the reasoning only needs their order, and any table where cutting alone beats holding together and both cutting beats being undercut has the same answer. Repeated play can change it; a single round cannot.

2 · A deck says a platform's value will quadruple when its members double, "by Metcalfe's law". What is the honest response?

The mechanism, that a bigger network is worth more to each member, is real. The exponent is the disputed part, and the difference between the two claims at one doubling, four times against 2.2 times, is exactly the difference between two valuations of the same deck.

3 · A platform says its switching costs are a moat. Which observation weakens the claim most?

Reviews, notice periods and proprietary formats are bricks in the wall; they strengthen the claim. Multi-homing is the open door: where sellers and buyers are already on two platforms, the switching cost is never paid, and the moat is a wall with a queue walking through it.
